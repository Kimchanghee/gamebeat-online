## Why should shape come before the largest DPI number?

Start with what is uncomfortable or limiting about your current mouse. Perhaps the rear hump presses against your palm, the shell is too narrow to hold without squeezing, or a side button requires you to shift your grip. Naming the problem gives you something useful to compare. Replacing a mouse solely because another model advertises a higher maximum DPI does not address any of those issues.

Logitech's gaming mouse guide describes palm, claw and fingertip grips and emphasizes hand size and comfort. Use those terms to describe your hold, not as rigid categories that dictate one correct product. Your contact points may change between a slow cursor movement and a large turn. A shape should remain controllable through the movements you actually make.

Compare dimensions with a mouse you know, but do not treat equal length and width as equal fit. Hump position, side curvature and button placement can make similarly sized shells feel different. Product photographs from the top and side are useful for screening; a fit check and workable return terms remain important when comfort is uncertain.

## How much desk space does your sensitivity need?

Measure the usable area after placing the keyboard, not the total width of the desk mat. The keyboard edge, monitor stand and charging cable can all intrude into the movement path. If you frequently lift and reset the mouse, include space for that movement rather than checking only whether the mouse can sit beside the keyboard.

Lower sensitivity generally means moving farther for the same in-game turn. Higher sensitivity reduces the required distance but changes the precision demanded of small hand movements. Neither is a universal skill level. Choose a setting you can control comfortably and consistently in the games you play, with enough room for the largest movements those games require.

A smaller keyboard may reclaim useful space without requiring a new mouse. Before buying, try temporarily moving the keyboard and clearing the surrounding area. The [keyboard switch and layout guide](/guides/keyboard-switch-layout/) explains what you give up when removing the number pad or function row. A layout change is only useful if your daily shortcuts still work comfortably.

## What do DPI and in-game sensitivity actually help you decide?

Logitech describes DPI as the mouse sensitivity setting and highlights adjustable DPI for different uses. The practical buying question is whether the device provides a usable adjustment range and a convenient way to select it. The largest value printed on the box is not a target you must use.

The game also has its own sensitivity controls. Record the mouse setting and the game setting together before changing either. If you alter both at once, it becomes harder to identify what improved control or caused overshooting. Start from a familiar setup and change one variable at a time, then use the same repeatable movement to compare the result.

Do not assume that an identical sensitivity number produces the same turning distance in every game. Keep notes per game rather than copying a professional player's number without the rest of their setup. A useful personal check is whether you can make a comfortable large turn and a small correction without running into the keyboard or repeatedly straining to reach a target.

## How should you interpret polling rate claims?

Polling rate describes how frequently the mouse reports to the computer. Logitech gives the example that 1,000 Hz means 1,000 reports per second. The reciprocal is a nominal one-millisecond reporting interval, which is arithmetic rather than a measured total input delay.

Do not equate that interval with the time between moving your hand and seeing the completed action on screen. The game, computer and display are also part of that path. A higher advertised report rate alone cannot establish a particular end-to-end result on your system, and this guide does not claim a measured advantage for any model.

Check which connection and software settings enable the advertised rate. Ask whether a particular receiver is included or sold separately and whether the supported operating system matches yours. Prefer a documented configuration you can actually use over a headline specification whose requirements are unclear.

## Which buttons and connection features are worth paying for?

Map the actions you regularly use before deciding how many extra buttons you need. A crowded side panel may suit repeated ability bindings, but it adds little if you cannot distinguish the buttons without shifting your hand. Left-handed users should check the side-button arrangement, not only a description of the shell as symmetrical.

For wired models, think about cable routing and drag across the desk edge. For wireless models, check receiver storage, charging or battery replacement, and whether the documented wired mode meets your needs. Wireless freedom and charging responsibility arrive together; neither makes the entire category better for every desk.

If you need assignments across multiple computers, confirm whether settings are stored on the device or require an application running on each computer. Manufacturer software availability matters on work-managed machines and unsupported operating systems. A programmable label alone does not establish portability of your configuration.

## What should you check during the return window?

Use the normal desk position and familiar game settings first. Check reach to every required button, unintended clicks while lifting, comfortable grip during longer sessions and consistent behavior on your mouse surface. Keep the first comparison simple so that a new shape is not confused with several simultaneous software changes.

Stop treating discomfort as a feature you must adapt to. Revisit the workstation arrangement with the [chair-fit checklist](/guides/gaming-chair-fit/) and compare the mouse against the specific problem you wrote down initially. Choose the candidate that solves that problem while meeting your connection and control needs, not the candidate with the longest specification list.
