## Where should the screen sit relative to your eyes?

Begin with the way you sit when supported and relaxed, then place the monitor around that position. OSHA generally recommends a viewing distance between 20 and 40 inches, or 50 and 100 centimetres, measured from your eyes to the front of the screen. It also emphasizes being able to read text with the head and torso upright and the back supported.

The range is a starting point, not a compulsory target that overrides clear vision. A large display, small text and individual vision needs can change the useful arrangement. If you can meet a measurement only by leaning forward, pushing your head back or reaching awkwardly for the keyboard, the complete workstation still needs adjustment.

Measure from the position you actually use. Desk depth and eye-to-screen distance are not the same measurement, because the chair position, stand footprint and display placement affect the result. Try the arrangement with a normal menu or document as well as a game. A beautiful full-screen image can hide the fact that small text is difficult to read.

## How high should the monitor be?

OSHA recommends placing the top of the monitor at or slightly below eye level, with the screen centre normally about 15 to 20 degrees below horizontal eye level. The aim is to avoid sustained awkward head and neck posture, not to align a bezel with a decorative shelf or another piece of equipment.

Look for what your body is doing while reading. If your chin lifts to see the upper part of the display, examine whether the screen is too high. If you repeatedly collapse forward toward the lower part, check height, distance and text size together. One isolated adjustment may solve one issue while creating another, so reassess after each change.

Vision correction matters. OSHA notes that bifocal users may tilt their head backward to look through the lower portion of their lenses and may need a lower monitor position. Do not force the general height recommendation on that situation. Discuss computer-viewing needs with an appropriate eye-care professional rather than changing prescribed lenses based on a gaming setup guide.

## What should you do when the desk is too shallow?

Start by measuring the space the stand actually uses and how far the display can safely move back. OSHA identifies inadequate desk depth as a reason a comfortable viewing position can be hard to achieve. Moving a desk away from a wall can create clearance behind the screen, but only if the equipment remains safely supported and the new arrangement does not obstruct the room.

A different stand or arm may help use the available surface, but its mounting compatibility, supported weight and desk requirements need separate verification. Do not assume that any clamp can be fitted to any tabletop. Follow the equipment instructions and avoid improvised stacks or unstable supports that turn a posture adjustment into a falling-object risk.

Preserve space for the keyboard and mouse while moving the monitor. Pushing the chair far back can increase viewing distance while leaving the arms reaching forward. That is not a complete solution. The screen, inputs and seated position must work together; this guide focuses on the screen rather than repeating the seat-depth and armrest decisions in the [chair-fit guide](/guides/gaming-chair-fit/).

## Should the main monitor be centred or placed to one side?

OSHA recommends placing the monitor directly in front so the head, neck and torso face forward, and warns against sustained head rotation. For a gaming desk, identify the primary visual task first. If the main game occupies one screen and chat occupies another, place the main screen for the activity you spend most time doing rather than centring the gap between the displays for symmetry.

An occasional glance and a sustained viewing task are different demands. If you regularly turn to a secondary display for long periods, reconsider the arrangement for that activity. You might move the task window or reposition equipment rather than maintaining the same twisted posture because the cables were originally routed that way.

OSHA gives a limit of 35 degrees to either side for monitor placement, but this is not a reason to target the extreme. A straightforward position that avoids sustained twisting is the useful goal. No single dual-monitor angle is established here for every person, screen size or desk. Check the tasks and the actual comfortable viewing position instead.

## How can you reduce glare without compromising posture?

OSHA's quick guidance recommends placing the monitor perpendicular to a window. Consider both daylight and overhead lights, because a setup that works in the evening may reflect a bright window during the day. Inspect the screen from your seated viewing position, not from above the desk while standing.

Adjust position and lighting before making a habit of leaning around a reflection. OSHA notes that a strongly tilted screen can distort what you see and that tilting back can catch overhead glare. The screen should generally be perpendicular to your line of sight; its guidance describes a tilt of no more than about 10 to 20 degrees in typical arrangements.

Text clarity is another part of the same check. If menus are too small, adjust the relevant scaling or text setting rather than automatically pulling your face closer. The [resolution and refresh guide](/guides/monitor-resolution-refresh-input/) explains why scaling and resolution are different choices. A higher refresh setting does not correct a poorly placed display or a reflection across the text.

## How should you check the setup after making changes?

Use a normal mix of activities, including reading, gaming and looking at secondary information. Note whether you drift forward, raise your chin or rotate for a sustained task. Change one factor at a time and reassess. A single photograph of an apparently tidy desk cannot establish that the arrangement remains comfortable during use.

OSHA advises looking away periodically, focusing on farther objects, blinking and alternating with non-computer activities to rest the eyes. Build those changes into the session rather than expecting a correctly positioned monitor to remove the need for breaks. This guide does not promise to prevent pain or diagnose eye symptoms.

If discomfort persists, seek appropriate professional advice instead of repeatedly buying accessories. The purchase question is narrower: does the equipment let you adjust distance, height, tilt and rotation safely for your needs? Useful adjustment range and readable text matter more than copying the exact screen position from someone else's gaming photograph.
