A keyboard that costs this much invites one quick question: does the headline speed come out of the box on its own? For the ROG Azoth Extreme the answer depends on a small accessory, and that is worth knowing before you order a premium board.

We have not used this keyboard, measured its latency or timed its battery. The checks below come from the ASUS product page, the ASUS eShop specification list and separately attributed reviews from Digital Trends, PCMag and RTINGS. No customer review, rating or product price is used here.

## Does the Azoth Extreme reach 8000 Hz without the Polling Rate Booster?
No. ASUS describes an 8000 Hz report rate together with the ROG Polling Rate Booster, and its eShop table lists 8000 Hz for both the wired and 2.4 GHz connections with that accessory. The same table lists the booster in the box contents. Digital Trends explains the practical split: the included Omni receiver on its own gives 1000 Hz, and the booster is what raises it.

That means the headline number is a configuration, not a default. Plan for the extra piece to occupy a USB port, and read the listing to confirm the box you are buying includes it. We have not opened a unit to confirm the contents, so rely on the ASUS contents list and on the selected option on the page.

Keep the figure in proportion too. A polling rate describes how often the keyboard reports to the computer. It does not by itself promise better aim, and nobody here has measured game results. RTINGS reports very low and consistent latency with wireless 8000 Hz polling, which is a separately attributed measurement, not our test.

## How long can the wireless battery run?
ASUS states over 1600 hours of battery life in ROG SpeedNova 2.4 GHz wireless mode. Digital Trends adds that the figure is with the OLED display and RGB lighting turned off, and that the higher polling rate reduces runtime compared with the original Azoth. Treat 1600 hours as a best-case rating rather than a promise for a lit, screen-on setup.

If you intend to keep lighting and the OLED active, expect a shorter interval between charges. This guide does not estimate how short, because we have not logged any runtime. The keyboard can also be used wired over the 2 m braided USB-C to USB-A cable listed by ASUS, which is useful when the battery runs low.

## Which connections and systems does it support?
ASUS lists wired USB, Bluetooth 5.1 and 2.4 GHz RF. The ROG Omni Receiver can link one compatible keyboard and one compatible mouse through a single USB receiver, and ASUS notes that an Omni-compatible device can only be paired with one receiver at a time. The eShop lists macOS 10.11 or later and Windows 11 as supported systems, with Armoury Crate as the software.

Our [keyboard switch and layout guide](/guides/keyboard-switch-layout/) explains why a 75% layout drops some keys that a full-size board keeps. ASUS also lists all keys as programmable, but we have not tested the software. CanBuyOrNot calls Armoury Crate clunky but workable, which is a point to weigh if you dislike configuration utilities.

## What do hot-swap sockets, the OLED screen and the gasket mount add?
The listing for this option names ROG NX Snow Linear switches and PBT keycaps. ASUS describes pre-lubed, hot-swappable ROG NX switches, so you can change switches without soldering. Check that the switch and keycap options shown on the Amazon page match what you want, because the same product page offers other switch types and other Azoth models.

The keyboard also has a full-color OLED touchscreen with a three-way knob, a carbon-fiber positioning plate, three dampening layers and an adjustable gasket mount with a paddle switch for two typing feels. PCMag praised the two distinct key feels and the build, but said the touchscreen is tricky to swipe and tap. Our [monitor and input guide](/guides/monitor-resolution-refresh-input/) is a better place to check whether the rest of your setup can make use of a fast polling rate.

## Who should skip the ROG Azoth Extreme?
Skip it if you want a lightweight keyboard: ASUS lists about 2200 g with the wrist rest and 332 x 139 x 40 mm. Skip it if you do not want to manage extra USB hardware, or if a screen-on lighting setup with long battery life is your priority.

Reviewers were consistent that the main drawback is cost. PCMag called its price the only major flaw, Tom's Hardware said it is not worth it at the launch price, and Digital Trends said it would not spend that much on a keyboard. We list no price here because it changes, but compare the current figure against a hot-swappable board without the screen if you are only after typing feel.

The useful conclusion is narrow. This is a well-reviewed, heavy 75% board whose 8000 Hz rating needs the booster and whose battery rating is a best case. Choose it for the build, the typing feel and the customization, not because a single specification line promises more speed than the setup actually delivers.
